Why I paused the site-control checker I built for PJM's interconnection queue

Why I paused Zonevex, software that checked site control against interconnection-queue rules: the commit history, the outreach result, PJM's own AI review tool, and what I would build instead.

TL;DR. I built Zonevex, software that checks whether a developer’s site control would hold up against an RTO’s interconnection-queue rules: 836 commits on its main branch1, almost all of them between February and June 20262. After a silent summer I paused it in November. Two reasons: PJM, the RTO my free toolkits were built for, was already using an AI tool from Tapestry to review application documents3, and I never found a channel to the people who buy this kind of software. The first is documented. The second is my judgment, not a measurement. The result was no paying customers, one booked call and one reply to 2,454 cold emails4.

Why this matters. It cost me about 150 hours and $185 to find out5. If you are thinking about compliance software for grid and power, the engineering is the part you can control, and it is the part I got furthest on.

As of 2026-11-13.

What I believed#

I framed Zonevex from the start as a time-capped bet on distribution, not a product bet, with a falsification gate set for October 2026. I already knew the buyers were slow: utilities and project developers tend to buy from vendors they have met, and enterprise cycles run long against a solo founder’s runway.

What I did#

Zonevex took lease and easement PDFs. Claude Vision extracted the fields, a parser turned legal descriptions into geometry, PostGIS computed parcel coverage, and per-RTO rules held in a database table produced the report. The code stayed in a private repository: first commit 2026-02-21, last 2026-10-226. Around it I built a marketing site, free PJM toolkits and 25 blog posts7.

2 Commits per month on the Zonevex main branch. Counts commits, not effort.

The activity stopped after June. The repository has no commits in July, August or September, and a handful in October2. The chart excludes the pause documents written in November. Three things overlapped over the summer: I decided to stop, other priorities took the time, and I was waiting on outreach.

The October commits were all between October 20 and 22 and were cleanup, not new product: removing prices from the public pages and the app, correcting claims in my HyperQ post, adding a PJM large-load post, fixing the web deploy workflow, and a first draft of a specification for an open interconnection rules register8.

In October I tried something smaller than selling: short, sourced notes that told a developer where two public records disagreed about one of their projects. No pitch. I sent a few through company web forms to see whether anyone engaged.

What happened#

The outcome was thin. Only some people downloaded the free calculator and toolkits, mostly from private equity firms. One call was booked through Cal.com with a founder in the site-control field. I sent 2,454 cold emails, one person replied, and they said they did not need it4.

My read: one reply from that many emails says something about my targeting and message. It does not show that the market has no need.

PJM’s reformed Cycle 1 required proof of site control, and 811 projects applied by the window’s close on April 279. PJM said its staff were already using HyperQ, a tool from Google’s Tapestry, to identify and assess sections of application documents for further review3.

Tapestry’s own account is more specific. HyperQ evaluates each bundle of agreements against four site-control elements: term, conveyance, exclusivity and minimum acreage10. Everything here is vendor-reported. Tapestry says PJM’s experts “retained full control over all final determinations”11.

HyperQ was not available to developers when I checked. Tapestry lists developer pre-submission access as a future item with no date12. That is a plan, not a product, and it points at the same ground I was working: telling a developer whether their site control will pass before they file.

Why it failed#

My best explanation, offered as opinion: the limit was distribution and procurement, not the product. New grid and power technology lacks trusted pilot and procurement channels, because of locked-down data, liability, long vendor cycles and busy decision-makers.

The second factor is documented but smaller than it sounds. A vendor aligned with the RTO’s own reviewers covers the first-pass check. It does not, as far as I could tell, cover a developer’s multi-RTO portfolio, land workflow or title work, so it narrows the opening rather than closing it. This is my judgment from reading the vendor page, not a test.

Measured cause: none. I have no data that separates “no demand” from “no channel to find it.” What would separate them is a paid pilot offered through a channel I could actually reach, and I did not run one.

What I’d do instead#

I would start with point-in-time capture: engines that record the state of interconnection queues with dated timestamps, beginning with site control. And I would build them as open-source tooling first, not as business-to-business sales. That is my judgment, not a tested result.

Limits of this result#

  • What I didn’t test: a paid pilot, any RTO other than PJM as a first customer, and whether the free notes changed anyone’s behavior.
  • Could this work elsewhere? Yes, under conditions I wrote down when I paused it: a buyer need the incumbents will not serve, inbound interest with a budget or a pilot, a rule change that opens a wedge, or a distribution channel I can reach. Any one of those would change my answer.

An open question. Would a developer pay for a second opinion on site control from someone who is not the RTO’s reviewer, and if so, who inside the company signs for it?
